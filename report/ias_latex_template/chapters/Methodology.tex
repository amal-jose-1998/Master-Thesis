\chapter{Methodology}
\label{chap:methodology}

This chapter presents the methodology used to model and predict short-horizon \ac{HDV} maneuvers in highway traffic. 
Starting from raw vehicle trajectories in the highD dataset, the pipeline constructs a vehicle-centric representation, 
extracts interaction and risk-related features, summarizes them over short time windows, and uses the resulting observation sequences 
to train and evaluate the proposed \ac{DBN}.

\par In highway traffic, a similar traffic situation can lead to several plausible driver responses. 
For example, when approaching a slower vehicle, a driver may brake and continue following or change lanes to overtake, depending on the surrounding traffic context. 
The resulting maneuver is therefore uncertain. To capture this uncertainty, the proposed method adopts a probabilistic model.

\par The goal is to predict a probability distribution over discrete high-level action classes, such as acceleration, braking, lane changes, and stable following, rather than an exact future trajectory.   
To achieve this, the observation space combines ego-motion variables with contextual information from 
surrounding vehicles and risk-related quantities.

\par The remainder of this chapter describes the overall framework in \autoref{sec:proposed_framework}, 
the dataset in \autoref{sec:dataset}, the preprocessing and feature-engineering pipeline in \autoref{sec:data_preparation}, 
the proposed \ac{DBN} model in \autoref{sec:proposed_dbn_model}, the training procedure in \autoref{sec:training_validation},  
the inference and evaluation procedure in \autoref{sec:inference_evaluation}, 
and the semantic interpretation of latent states in \autoref{sec:semantic_interpretation_latent_states}.


\section{Proposed Framework}
\label{sec:proposed_framework}

The framework converts raw highD trajectory data into temporal observations for action prediction.
Its main stages are data preparation, \ac{DBN} training, and inference.

\par The pipeline starts from frame-level trajectories and transforms them into a consistent vehicle-centric representation.
Based on the ego vehicle and its local traffic environment, additional features are derived to capture interaction context, 
lane-related information, and risk-related properties such as \ac{THW} and \ac{TTC}.
These steps provide a compact description of the current driving situation beyond the ego vehicle.

\par Action prediction depends on both the current traffic state and its recent evolution. For this reason, frame-level signals are aggregated 
over short sliding windows to form a window-based sequence representation. 
Each window becomes one observation for a \ac{DBN} with two discrete latent variables: a style state and an action state.
The engineered window features are linked to these latent states through the emission model, while temporal behavior is captured by the state transition model.

\par Model parameters are estimated from the training data using an \ac{EM}-based learning procedure. 
Each windowized observation sequence corresponds to a single vehicle trajectory. 
To prevent leakage across the training, validation, and test sets, the data are partitioned at the vehicle level into disjoint subsets. 
Specifically, the split contains 35,318 training vehicles (70\%), 5,045 validation vehicles (10\%), and 10,092 test vehicles (20\%).
The training set is used for parameter estimation, the validation set is used to monitor validation log-likelihood and to apply early stopping during \ac{EM} training, 
and the test set is reserved for the final evaluation. 
After training, inference is performed on the held-out test set to estimate the posterior distribution over latent states and to predict the distribution over future maneuvers.

\par An overview of the complete framework is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:proposed_framework}. 
The individual stages are described in detail in the following sections.

\section{Dataset}
\label{sec:dataset}
This thesis uses the highD dataset \cite{krajewski2018highddatasetdronedataset}, a naturalistic highway-traffic dataset recorded from drone videos on German highways.
Its aerial perspective provides frame-level trajectories together with surrounding-vehicle information, making it well suited for the analysis of human driving behavior in highway scenarios.

\par The full highD dataset contains 60 recordings collected at several highway locations. 
In this work, only the first 25 recordings with three lanes in each driving direction are used. 
This restriction provides a more uniform road geometry and traffic setting across the selected subset, which supports more consistent behavior modeling.
Figure~\ref{fig:highd_scene_example} shows an example traffic scene from the selected subset and illustrates the multi-lane highway layout together with the surrounding-vehicle 
context available for each target vehicle.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{images/dataset_illustration.png}
    \caption[Example traffic scene from a selected highD subset]{Example traffic scene from a selected highD subset. The red box denotes the ego vehicle, while the blue and green boxes represent other vehicles traveling in the two opposite driving directions.}
    \label{fig:highd_scene_example}
\end{figure}

\par For each vehicle, highD provides frame-level kinematic variables such as longitudinal and lateral position, velocity, and acceleration.
It also provides lane-related information, driving-direction labels, and identifiers for neighboring vehicles in 8 surrounding positions.
In addition, the dataset provides traffic-safety indicators such as \ac{TTC} and \ac{THW}, which support the construction of interaction and risk-related features. 
These signals form the basis for the preprocessing and feature-engineering pipeline described in \autoref{sec:data_preparation}.

\par For the selected subset, 50,543 vehicle trajectories are available after loading the chosen recordings. 
With a window length of \(W=50\) frames, 50,455 trajectories satisfy \(T \geq W\), while only 88 are shorter than the required window length.  
Most trajectories are long enough for temporal modeling: half have about 349 frames, and 80\% fall between 280 and 466 frames.   
This indicates that the selected subset is sufficiently large and temporally rich for constructing window-based observation sequences.

\section{Data Preparation and Preprocessing}
\label{sec:data_preparation}






\subsection{Preprocessing Pipeline}
\label{subsec:preprocessing_pipeline}
The raw highD files are first merged into a single per-frame representation that combines trajectory data with vehicle-level and recording-level metadata. 
Since the dataset contains multiple recordings, each trajectory is identified by the pair (\texttt{recording\_id}, \texttt{vehicle\_id}).
Vehicle-center coordinates are then computed from the original bounding-box positions to provide a consistent geometric reference. 


\par The selected recordings contain traffic moving in two opposite roadway directions. 
In the raw data, forward longitudinal motion has opposite signs across these directions, and the same inconsistency affects related kinematic variables.  
Therefore, the raw signals from highD cannot be used directly as a single consistent representation. 
A set of plots illustrating this effect is provided in Appendix~\ref{appendix:raw_plots}. 
To resolve this issue, a vehicle-centric normalization step is applied using the driving-direction label. 
This transformation makes the sign conventions for longitudinal and lateral motion consistent across both traffic directions. 
As a result, the same maneuver is represented with the same sign convention regardless of the original roadway direction.


\par After these normalization steps, the processed dataframe provides a consistent per-frame representation that is used for feature engineering and subsequent windowization.

\par The lane-change analysis further highlights an important property of the dataset.
As shown in \autoref{fig:lc_frame_distribution}, almost all frames belong to the lane-keeping class, while left and right lane changes occur only rarely.

First, longitudinal and lateral kinematics provide complementary information for maneuver prediction. 
Second, trajectory-level variability contains useful information beyond single-frame values.

These findings guided the final feature design for the proposed \ac{DBN}.





\subsection{Feature Engineering}
\label{subsec:feature_engineering}
In the feature-engineering stage, the available neighboring positions include the front, rear, left-front, left-side, left-rear, right-front, right-side, and right-rear slots.
For each available neighbor, binary existence flags are created and relative kinematic quantities are derived in the form of relative longitudinal distance, relative lateral distance, relative longitudinal velocity, and relative lateral velocity.
Missing neighbors remain marked through the corresponding existence indicators.

\par In the final observation vector, only the subset of neighboring vehicles shown in Figure~\ref{fig:neighbor_slots} is retained.
This choice makes the model focus on the vehicles that are most relevant for immediate vehicle-following constraints in the current lane.
It also captures the local traffic state of the adjacent lanes while keeping the feature space compact and less complex.
Rear and rear-diagonal neighbors may also provide useful information, but they are excluded here to keep the observation space lower-dimensional.

\par In addition to interaction features, lane-related and risk-related quantities are derived.
Distances to the left and right lane boundaries are computed from the recording metadata and the ego-vehicle center position.
The lane-change indicator is obtained from changes in lane ID and represented as a ternary variable corresponding to lane change to left, no lane change, and lane change to right.
The front-interaction safety signals provided by highD are mapped to the variables \texttt{front\_thw} and \texttt{front\_ttc}.
Furthermore, ego-vehicle jerk is computed from the frame-to-frame change in longitudinal and lateral acceleration.

\subsection{Window-Based Representation}
\label{subsec:window_based_representation}
The proposed model operates on short temporal windows rather than on individual frames. 
Each vehicle trajectory is therefore converted into a sequence of overlapping windows, as illustrated in \autoref{fig:window_based_representation}.
This allows the model to capture short-term temporal dynamics and local interaction context instead of relying only on instantaneous frame values.

\par Let $W$ denote the window length and $S$ the stride between consecutive windows. 
In this work, $W=50$ frames and $S=10$ frames are used. 
Since highD is recorded at 25\,Hz, each window covers approximately 2\,s of history, while consecutive windows start 0.4\,s apart. 
A 2\,s observation horizon is also consistent with prior highway maneuver prediction work on highD \cite{3161785}. 
For a trajectory of length $T$ frames, overlapping windows are extracted every $S$ frames as long as $T \geq W$. 

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{images/windowize.png}
    \caption{Illustration of the sliding-window representation used in this work.}
    \label{fig:window_based_representation}
\end{figure}

\par Each window is then aggregated into one observation vector.  
Depending on the variable, the aggregation uses the most recent value, a minimum over the window, a temporal slope, sign-based fractions, percentile-based summaries, 
binary event indicators, or fractions of valid or available frames. For neighbor-dependent variables, the summaries are computed only when the corresponding neighbor is present.
The complete list of window-level features used in the final observation vector is provided in Appendix~\ref{appendix:window_features}.





\section{Proposed DBN Model}
\label{sec:proposed_dbn_model}
The proposed model represents driver behavior through two discrete latent variables: a style state $S_t$ and an action state $A_t$. 
The style state captures a broader driving regime, while the action state represents the maneuver at the current time step. 
The observation vector $O_t$ corresponds to the window-level features. 


\subsection{Latent State Structure}
\label{subsec:latent_state_structure}
The model uses a factorized latent representation. 
As shown in \autoref{fig:proposed_dbn_structure}, the style state evolves as its own Markov chain, while the action state 
depends on both the previous action state and the current style state.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \input{figures/sharedDBN}
    \caption{Factorized latent structure of the proposed DBN.}
    \label{fig:proposed_dbn_structure}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Transition Model}
\label{subsec:transition_model}
The transition model specifies how the latent states evolve from one time step to the next.
The initial distribution is modeled as:
\begin{align}
p(S_1 = s_1) &= \bm{\pi}^{(s)}_{s_1} \\
p(A_1 = a_1 \mid S_1 = s_1) &= \bm{\Pi}^{(a)}_{s_1, a_1}
\end{align}

For later time steps, with t=2, \dots, T, the transition model is factorized as:
\begin{align}
p(S_t = s_t \mid S_{t-1} = s_{t-1}) &= \bm{A}^{(s)}_{s_{t-1}, s_t}\\
p(A_t = a_t \mid A_{t-1} = a_{t-1}, S_t = s_t) &= \mathcal{A}^{(a)}_{s_t, a_{t-1}, a_t}
\end{align}

Here, $\bm{\pi}^{(s)}$ is the initial style-probability vector, $\bm{\Pi}^{(a)}$ is the conditional initial action matrix, $\bm{A}^{(s)}$ is the style transition matrix, and $\mathcal{A}^{(a)}$ is the action transition tensor.

This factorization reflects the assumption that the style state evolves more gradually, 
while the current maneuver depends on both the recent maneuver history and the current driving regime. 


\subsection{Emission Model Variants}
\label{subsec:emission_model_variants}
At each time step, the emission model specifies the conditional probability of the window-level observation vector given the latent state pair \((S_t, A_t)\).  
Two alternative emission formulations are considered in this work, as illustrated in \autoref{fig:emission_variants}. 
Both share the same latent transition structure and differ only in how the observation model is parameterized.   
In both cases, the observation vector is split into continuous and binary components, modeled by diagonal Gaussian and Bernoulli distributions, respectively.


\subsubsection{Product-of-Experts Emission}
\label{subsubsec:poe_emission}
In the \ac{PoE} formulation, the observation likelihood is decomposed into two experts: one conditioned on the style state and one conditioned on the action state. 
Their contributions are combined multiplicatively and then normalized:
\begin{equation}
p(O_t \mid S_t=s_t, A_t=a_t) = \frac{p_s(O_t \mid S_t=s_t; \theta_{s_t})\,p_a(O_t \mid A_t=a_t; \theta_{a_t})}{Z_{s_t,a_t}}
\end{equation}
Here, $\theta_{s_t}$ denotes the parameters of the style expert associated with style state $s_t$, and $\theta_{a_t}$ denotes the parameters of the action expert associated with action state $a_t$. 
Each parameter set contains the Gaussian parameters for the continuous features and the Bernoulli parameters for the binary features. 
The term $Z_{s_t,a_t}$ is the partition function that normalizes the product of experts for state pair $(s_t,a_t)$, 
ensuring a valid probability distribution, as in standard \ac{PoE} formulations \cite{Hinton2002}.

\subsubsection{Hierarchical Emission}
\label{subsubsec:hierarchical_emission}
In the hierarchical formulation, a single emission distribution is learned directly for each joint latent-state pair:
\begin{equation}
p(O_t \mid S_t=s_t, A_t=a_t) = p(O_t \mid S_t=s_t, A_t=a_t; \theta_{s_t,a_t})
\end{equation}
Here, $\theta_{s_t,a_t}$ denotes the parameters of the emission distribution associated with the joint state pair $(s_t, a_t)$. 
As in the \ac{PoE} case, these parameters include the Gaussian terms for the continuous features and the Bernoulli terms for the binary features.
This formulation gives the model greater flexibility for each joint latent-state pair, at the cost of a larger number of emission parameters.



\subsection{Modeling Assumptions}
\label{subsec:modeling_assumptions}

The proposed \ac{DBN} is modeled based on the following assumptions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{First-order temporal dependency:}  
    The style state \(S_t\) depends only on the previous style state \(S_{t-1}\).  
    The action state \(A_t\) depends only on the previous action state \(A_{t-1}\) and the current style state \(S_t\).  
    This gives a first-order Markov structure for the latent process.
    \item \textbf{Observation dependence on the current latent state only:}  
    The observation vector \(O_t\) depends only on the latent variables at the same time step.  
    Once \(S_t\) and \(A_t\) are known, the current observation is assumed to be conditionally independent of past observations and past latent states.
    \item \textbf{Window-level observation representation:}  
    Each model time step corresponds to one windowed observation vector rather than one raw frame from the dataset. 
    \item \textbf{Split into continuous and binary features:}  
    The observation vector is divided into continuous and binary components.  
    Among the window features, the lane-change indicator variables are binary, while the remaining features are treated as continuous.
    \item \textbf{Naive Bayes-style conditional independence:}  
    Given the relevant latent state pair, features are assumed conditionally independent within each observation.
    \item \textbf{Diagonal Gaussian assumption for continuous features:}  
    Continuous features are modeled using diagonal Gaussian distributions.  
    This corresponds to conditionally independent continuous feature dimensions given the latent states.
    \item \textbf{Bernoulli assumption for binary features:}  
    Binary features are modeled using Bernoulli distributions.  
    The binary dimensions are treated as conditionally independent given the latent states.
    \item \textbf{Missing-feature handling:}  
    Some features may be undefined at certain time steps, especially interaction-related quantities when neighboring vehicles are absent.  
    Such values are treated as missing and are excluded from the corresponding likelihood terms through masking, rather than being interpreted as informative values.
\end{itemize}





\section{Model Training}
\label{sec:training_validation}
As described in \autoref{sec:proposed_framework}, the windowized vehicle sequences are divided into training, validation, and test sets. 
This section explains how the observation sequences are prepared, how the model parameters are initialized, 
and how the proposed \ac{DBN} is trained and monitored during learning.

\subsection{Feature Scaling}
Before training, the continuous window-level features are normalized to make them comparable in scale. 
This is necessary because the observation vector contains different physical quantities, such as velocity, 
acceleration, lane-relative distances, and interaction-related measures, which naturally have different numerical ranges. 
Without scaling, features with larger magnitudes could dominate the likelihood computation and make parameter estimation less stable.

\par In the implemented pipeline, z-score scaling statistics, namely the mean and standard deviation, are computed from the training observations and then reused unchanged 
for the training, validation, and test splits. 
The same transformation is also
applied during inference to any new observation sequence.   
Only continuous features are normalized in this way. The binary lane-change indicators, namely \texttt{lc\_left\_present} and \texttt{lc\_right\_present}, 
are kept in their original form.
These scaling values are fixed preprocessing quantities and are not part of the learned emission model. 




\subsection{Parameter Initialization}
Before iterative learning begins, the model parameters must be assigned initial values. 
These include the initial-state and transition probabilities of the \ac{DBN}, as well as the parameters of the emission model. 
This step is important because the \ac{EM} algorithm is sensitive to its starting point and may otherwise converge to a poor local optimum. 

\par In the implemented framework, the emission model is initialized from the scaled training observations using K-means clustering. 
For the continuous part of the observation vector, the cluster centers and cluster variances are used as the initial Gaussian emission parameters. 
For the binary part, the initial Bernoulli probabilities are derived from the clustered observations. 
This provides a more informative starting point than a purely random assignment.

\par The initial latent-state distribution and the transition probabilities are set separately.  
Depending on the experiment, different schemes can be used, including uniform, random, or sticky initialization. 
In the sticky case, self-transitions are assigned higher initial probability than transitions to different latent states.


\subsection{EM-Based Training}
After initialization, the \ac{DBN} is trained iteratively on the windowized observation sequences. 
At each iteration, the current model is applied to all training sequences to compute the posterior state probabilities and expected transition counts.  
These quantities are accumulated over all sequences and then used to update the model parameters. 
The procedure is repeated until the stopping criterion is met.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{EM-based training procedure}
\label{alg:em_training}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require scaled training and validation sequences, initialized model parameters
\Repeat
    \For{each training sequence}
        \State compute emission log-likelihoods
        \State run forward-backward inference
        \State accumulate posterior state probabilities and expected transition counts
    \EndFor
    \State update initial-state, transition, and emission parameters
    \State evaluate the current model on the validation set
\Until{stopping criterion is satisfied}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\par The re-estimation of the initial-state and transition parameters follows the \ac{EM} update equations introduced in \autoref{sec:parameter_learning}. 
The emission-parameter update depends on the chosen observation model.
The theoretical derivations are given in Appendix~\ref{appendix:emission_updates}.

\par In the hierarchical emission model, one emission distribution is learned directly for each joint latent-state pair \((S_t,A_t)\). 
After the E-step, the posterior probabilities of the joint states are used as weights for re-estimating the emission parameters. 
For the continuous features, posterior-weighted means and diagonal variances are computed. 
For the binary features, posterior-weighted Bernoulli probabilities are computed. 
Thus, the hierarchical emission parameters can be updated in closed form.  

\par In the \ac{PoE} emission model, because of the normalization term, the style and action emission parameters are coupled during learning. 
As a result, the M-step does not reduce to a closed-form update as in the hierarchical case. 
Instead, the \ac{PoE} emission parameters are updated by an inner gradient-based optimization loop within each EM iteration. 

\par Lane-change-related events are much rarer than lane-keeping behavior, as shown in \autoref{fig:lane_change_analysis}. 
For this reason, additional weighting was investigated during training so that rare observations could have a stronger influence on learning. 
This follows the general idea of cost-sensitive weighting for imbalanced data \cite{He2021WeightingRareEvents}.
In this case, lane-change-active time steps are upweighted using an inverse-frequency rule 
computed from the training data, with an upper clipping threshold to avoid excessively large weights.

\par Two weighting variants were considered. 
In the first variant, the weights are applied directly to the emission log-likelihood before forward-backward inference. 
Since multiplying a log-likelihood term by a positive weight is equivalent to raising the corresponding likelihood term to a power, 
this variant can be interpreted as a power-likelihood formulation \cite{holmes2017assigningvaluepowerlikelihood}. 
As a result, this variant modifies the posterior inference itself.

\par In the second variant, forward-backward pass is first carried out under the original model, and the weights are applied afterward to 
the posterior quantities used in the parameter update. 
This is closer to weighted-data \ac{EM}, in which observations contribute to parameter estimation with different strengths 
through weighted responsibilities and weighted update equations \cite{Gebru_2016}. 
In this case, the pass itself remains unweighted, and only the parameter update is weighted.

\par After each iteration, the model is evaluated on the validation set using the average validation log-likelihood per observation. 
Training stops when this value no longer improves meaningfully over several successive iterations. 


\section{Inference and Evaluation}
\label{sec:inference_evaluation}
After training, the model is applied to unseen sequences from the held-out test split.
Evaluation is carried out in two complementary settings. 
First, full-sequence inference is used to assess how well the trained \ac{DBN} explains unseen observation sequences. 
Second, causal online inference is used to predict the next latent state from observations available up to the current time step. 

\par Across the experimental study, multiple model configurations are first compared using generative evaluation metrics on unseen observation sequences.
Based on this comparison, a smaller set of candidate models is then selected for predictive evaluation in a strictly causal online setting.


\par For full-sequence analysis, inference is performed over the complete observation sequence to obtain the smoothed joint posterior distribution
$
p(S_t, A_t \mid O_{1:T})$, 
for each time step \(t\).    
Since this posterior is conditioned on the full sequence, including future observations relative to time \(t\), it is not a causal prediction setting. 
Instead, it is used to evaluate how well the model explains unseen data under the learned probabilistic structure.   
A better generative fit indicates that the model captures the temporal and contextual structure of driver behavior more faithfully. 
  
In this work, generative fit is assessed using likelihood-based measures such as average log-likelihood per time step and \ac{BIC}, 
together with posterior-based diagnostics such as entropy and state occupancy.

\par For predictive evaluation, inference is performed online. 
At time step \(t\), the current observation \(O_t\) is used to update the filtered belief \(p(S_t, A_t \mid O_{1:t})\), 
and the transition model is then used to compute the one-step-ahead predictive distribution.
Using the transition factorization from \autoref{subsec:transition_model}, this is given by:
\begin{align}
p(S_{t+1}=s', A_{t+1}=a' \mid O_{1:t})
&= \sum_{s_t} \sum_{a_t}
 p(S_t=s_t, A_t=a_t \mid O_{1:t})\, p(S_{t+1}=s' \mid S_t=s_t)\, 
\nonumber\\
&\quad\quad
p(A_{t+1}=a' \mid A_t=a_t, S_{t+1}=s')
\label{eq:predictive_dist}
\end{align}
The one-step prediction is then taken as the \ac{MAP} estimate of the joint latent state:
\begin{equation}
(\hat{S}_{t+1}, \hat{A}_{t+1})
=
\arg\max_{s,a}
p(S_{t+1}=s, A_{t+1}=a \mid O_{1:t})
\end{equation}

\par During online evaluation, each test trajectory is processed sequentially. 
The first few window-based observations are excluded from scoring so that the model can update its initial belief state based on the recent context of the current vehicle. 
After this warmup period, the model outputs a one-step-ahead prediction at each valid time step. 
These predictions are compared with rule-based reference labels obtained manually during semantic analysis and described in \autoref{sec:semantic_interpretation_latent_states}. 
Figure~\ref{fig:predictive_evaluation_pipeline} illustrates the causal prediction and evaluation procedure used in this work.

\subsection{Generative Evaluation Metrics}
\label{subsec:generative_evaluation_metrics}
\subsubsection{Average Log-Likelihood per Time Step}

For a set of test vehicles indexed by $i$, the average log-likelihood per time step is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\bar{\ell}_{\mathrm{step}}
=
\frac{1}{\sum_i T_i}
\sum_i \log p\!\left(O^{(i)}_{1:T_i}\right)
\end{equation}
where $T_i$ is the length of the trajectory sequence $i$.

This metric measures how well the trained model explains unseen observation
sequences on average. Since total log-likelihood scales with sequence length,
normalizing by $\sum_i T_i$ makes the score comparable across trajectories of different lengths. Higher values, that is, values
that are less negative, indicate a better fit.
For the proposed \ac{DBN}, the likelihood of a sequence is obtained by
marginalizing over all latent style and action sequences:
\begin{equation}
p\!\left(O^{(i)}_{1:T_i}\right)
=
\sum_{s_{1:T_i}} \sum_{a_{1:T_i}}
p\!\left(O^{(i)}_{1:T_i}, S_{1:T_i}=s_{1:T_i}, A_{1:T_i}=a_{1:T_i}\right)
\end{equation}

In practice, this quantity is evaluated with the forward algorithm rather than
by explicit summation over all latent-state sequences \cite{rabiner1989tutorial,pml1Book}.

\par To complement the global average score, a normalized log-likelihood is also
computed for each vehicle trajectory:
\begin{equation}
\ell_i
=
\frac{1}{T_i}
\log p\!\left(O^{(i)}_{1:T_i}\right)
\end{equation}
The distribution of $\ell_i$ over the test split is then summarized using
percentiles such as the 5th, 50th, and 95th percentiles.

This diagnostic shows whether good average likelihood is obtained uniformly
across vehicles or is dominated by only a subset of trajectories.

\subsubsection{Bayesian Information Criterion}

To compare model variants with different numbers of free parameters, a complexity-penalized score based on the \ac{BIC} is also computed on the training set:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{BIC} = k \log N - 2 \log \hat{L}
\end{equation}
where $k$ is the number of free model parameters, $N=\sum_i T_i$ is the total
number of training time steps, and \(\hat{L}\) denotes the fitted likelihood on the training set used for model comparison. 
Lower \ac{BIC} values indicate a better trade-off
between goodness of fit and model complexity \cite{Schwarz1978EstimatingTD}.

For the proposed model,
\begin{equation}
k = k_{\pi} + k_{\mathrm{trans}} + k_{\mathrm{em}}
\end{equation}
With \(K_S\) style states, \(K_A\) action states, \(D_c\) continuous features, and \(D_b\) binary features, the number of free parameters is:
\begin{align}
k_{\pi} &= (K_S-1) + K_S(K_A-1), \\
k_{\mathrm{trans}} &= K_S(K_S-1) + K_S K_A (K_A-1)
\end{align}
For the hierarchical emission model:
\begin{equation}
k_{\mathrm{em}}^{\mathrm{hier}}
=
K_S K_A (2D_c + D_b),
\end{equation}
assuming diagonal Gaussian parameters for the continuous part and Bernoulli parameters for the binary part. 
For the \ac{PoE} emission model:
\begin{equation}
k_{\mathrm{em}}^{\mathrm{PoE}}
=
(K_S + K_A)(2D_c + D_b)
\end{equation}


\subsubsection{Posterior Entropy}

The certainty of the inferred latent-state assignments is analyzed through
normalized posterior entropy. 

The normalized joint entropy is defined as:
\begin{equation}
H_{\mathrm{joint}}^{(i)}(t)
=
-\frac{1}{\log(K_S K_A)}
\sum_{s=1}^{K_S}\sum_{a=1}^{K_A}
\gamma_t^{(i)}(s,a)\log \gamma_t^{(i)}(s,a),
\end{equation}
with the convention that $0\log 0 = 0$.
This normalization ensures that $
0 \le H_{\mathrm{joint}}^{(i)}(t) \le 1$.

The corresponding marginal style and action entropies are:
\begin{align}
H_{\mathrm{style}}^{(i)}(t)
&=
-\frac{1}{\log K_S}
\sum_{s=1}^{K_S}
\gamma_t^{(i)}(s)\log \gamma_t^{(i)}(s), \\
H_{\mathrm{action}}^{(i)}(t)
&=
-\frac{1}{\log K_A}
\sum_{a=1}^{K_A}
\gamma_t^{(i)}(a)\log \gamma_t^{(i)}(a)
\end{align}

Lower entropy indicates a more confident latent-state assignment, while higher
entropy indicates greater ambiguity \cite{CoverThomas2006}. 

\subsubsection{State Occupancy and Effective Number of States}

Posterior state occupancies are analyzed to determine how strongly the learned
latent states are used on the test split. The joint occupancy is defined as:
\begin{equation}
C_{\mathrm{joint}}(s,a)
=
\sum_i \sum_{t=1}^{T_i} \gamma_t^{(i)}(s,a)
\end{equation}

The marginal occupancies for style and action can be written directly from the joint posterior as:
\begin{align}
C_{\mathrm{style}}(s) &= \sum_i \sum_{t=1}^{T_i} \sum_{a=1}^{K_A} \gamma_t^{(i)}(s,a), \\
C_{\mathrm{action}}(a) &= \sum_i \sum_{t=1}^{T_i} \sum_{s=1}^{K_S} \gamma_t^{(i)}(s,a)
\end{align}
The corresponding normalized occupancy distributions are:
\begin{align}
p_{\mathrm{joint}}(s,a)
&=
\frac{C_{\mathrm{joint}}(s,a)}
{\sum_{s',a'} C_{\mathrm{joint}}(s',a')}, \\
p_{\mathrm{style}}(s)
&=
\frac{C_{\mathrm{style}}(s)}
{\sum_{s'} C_{\mathrm{style}}(s')}, \\
p_{\mathrm{action}}(a)
&=
\frac{C_{\mathrm{action}}(a)}
{\sum_{a'} C_{\mathrm{action}}(a')}
\end{align}
A concentrated occupancy distribution indicates that posterior mass is dominated by a small subset of states, 
whereas a flatter distribution indicates more balanced use of the state space.

To further quantify how many latent states are effectively used, the effective number of states can be computed from the entropy of the occupancy distribution:
\begin{equation}
N_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\mathrm{joint}}
=
\exp\!\left(
-\sum_{s=1}^{K_S}\sum_{a=1}^{K_A}
p_{\mathrm{joint}}(s,a)\log p_{\mathrm{joint}}(s,a)
\right)
\end{equation}
Analogous definitions are used for style and action. Larger values indicate that
posterior mass is spread across more latent states, while smaller values indicate that the model relies on only a few states \cite{Jost2006}.

\subsection{Predictive Evaluation Metrics}
\label{subsec:predictive_evaluation_metrics}
For the selected candidate models, predictive evaluation is carried out in a strictly causal setting after an initial warmup period. 
One-step-ahead predictions are assessed using exact next-step accuracy, horizon-based hit rate, and time-to-event. 

\subsubsection{Exact One-Step Prediction Accuracy}

For each valid prediction time, the model produces a one-step-ahead prediction of the joint latent state
\(
\hat{z}_{t+1} = (\hat{S}_{t+1}, \hat{A}_{t+1})
\)
after observing the sequence up to time $t$. 
The exact one-step prediction accuracy is defined as the fraction of scored time steps for which the predicted joint state matches the corresponding 
rule-based reference label exactly:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Accuracy}
=
\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{n=1}^{N}
\mathbf{1}\!\left[
\hat{z}_{t+1}^{(n)} = z_{t+1}^{(n)}
\right],
\end{equation}
where $N$ is the total scored prediction instances pooled over all trajectories.

\par Since the joint latent space is multi-class and class frequencies can be imbalanced, macro-averaged precision, recall, and F1-score are also computed from 
the joint-state confusion matrix. 
These metrics complement accuracy by giving equal importance to all classes rather than being dominated by the most frequent states \cite{Sokolova2009}.


\subsubsection{Hit Rate Within a Prediction Horizon}

Exact next-step accuracy is intentionally strict, since it counts a prediction as correct only when the predicted latent state appears immediately at time $t+1$. 
In behavior prediction, however, a forecast may still be useful when the predicted maneuver occurs a short time later. 
For this reason, a horizon-based hit metric is also used.

\par Let $\hat{z}_{t+1}$ denote the one-step-ahead predicted joint state. 
For a future horizon of $H$ steps, the prediction is counted as a hit if the predicted state appears at least once in the interval from $t+1$ to $t+H$:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Hit@H}(t)
=
\mathbf{1}\!\left[
\exists h \in \{1,\dots,H\}
:
z_{t+h} = \hat{z}_{t+1}
\right]
\end{equation}
The hit rate is then obtained by averaging this indicator over all scored prediction times.

\subsubsection{Time-to-Event}

To measure how early a correct prediction occurs within the prediction horizon, the \ac{TTE} is also computed. 
For a prediction made at time $t$, let $h^\ast(t)$ denote the first future step at which the predicted state appears:
\begin{equation}
h^\ast(t)
=
\min
\left\{
h \in \{1,\dots,H\}
:
z_{t+h} = \hat{z}_{t+1}
\right\}
\end{equation}
If no hit occurs within the horizon, the prediction is treated as a miss.

\par For successful hits, the time-to-event in seconds is given by
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{TTE}_{\mathrm{sec}}(t)
=
h^\ast(t)\,\Delta t,
\end{equation}
where $\Delta t$ denotes the duration of one window step. 
With window stride measured in frames and dataset frame rate measured in frames per second, this is:
\begin{equation}
\Delta t
=
\frac{\text{stride}}{\text{frame rate}}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Posterior-Based Interpretation of Individual Predictions}
\label{subsec:posterior_based_interpretation}

To interpret selected predictions locally, the model output can be decomposed into four parts: the prior belief, the emission likelihood, the posterior belief, and the one-step forecast. 
At a chosen time step $t$, the prior belief describes the latent-state distribution before the current observation is incorporated. 
The emission likelihood measures how compatible the current observation is with each latent-state pair. 
The posterior belief then represents the updated latent-state distribution after observing the current traffic context, and the one-step forecast propagates this posterior one step forward.

\par This decomposition makes it possible to analyse whether a prediction is driven mainly by the transition dynamics, by strong evidence from the current observation, or by a combination of both. 

\subsection{Computational Efficiency}
\label{subsec:computational_efficiency}

Since the intended application is online action prediction, computational efficiency is also evaluated. 
For this purpose, the trained model is benchmarked under several execution settings, including single-vehicle prediction, multi-vehicle sequential prediction, and batched prediction. 
These benchmarks are carried out both in a model-only setting, where precomputed window observations are given directly to the predictor, and in an end-to-end setting, where the full pipeline from frame-level input through windowization, scaling, 
belief update, and prediction is included. 
These results are used to assess whether the proposed model is suitable for short-horizon online prediction in an \ac{AV} context.




\section{Semantic Interpretation of Latent States}
\label{sec:semantic_interpretation_latent_states}

After training, an additional analysis is performed to interpret the learned latent states. 
The goal of this step is to relate the latent style and action states to characteristic traffic patterns observed in the data. 
For this purpose, the trained model is applied again to the training split.  
For each windowized observation sequence, posterior inference is carried out to obtain the joint posterior probabilities $\gamma_t(s,a)$.


\par The semantic interpretation is based on posterior-weighted feature statistics computed from the original observation features. 
For a selected feature \(o_t^{(f)}\) and a joint latent state pair \((s,a)\), the posterior-weighted mean is computed as:
\begin{equation}
\mu_{s,a,f}
=
\frac{\sum_n \sum_t \gamma_t^{(n)}(s,a)\,o_t^{(n,f)}}
{\sum_n \sum_t \gamma_t^{(n)}(s,a)}
\end{equation}
where $n$ indexes the analyzed vehicle trajectories. 
Posterior-weighted standard deviations are computed in the same way to describe the variability of each feature within the corresponding latent state pair. 
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{s,a,f}
=
\left(
\frac{\sum_n \sum_t \gamma_t^{(n)}(s,a)\left(o_t^{(n,f)}-\mu_{s,a,f}\right)^2}
{\sum_n \sum_t \gamma_t^{(n)}(s,a)}
\right)^{1/2}
\end{equation}
This produces a semantic feature profile for every joint state $(s,a)$, showing which motion, interaction, or risk-related properties are typically associated with that state.
